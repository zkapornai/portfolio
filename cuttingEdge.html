Cutting Edge

A paper-built charging dock that recreates the form and dimensions of an everyday charger through measuring, layered materials and hands-on making.

  • Making and prototyping
  • Hand construction
  • Paper engineering

01-02 / Reference · Measure

Start with the everyday object

This project documents the full process of recreating an everyday charger purely from paper materials. Measuring the original object provided a clear reference for the scale and geometry of the charging dock.

Record the proportions

Taking measurements first helped translate the charger's shape into a buildable paper model and kept the new form close to the original dimensions.

03-04 / Materials · Structure

Learn how the paper behaves

Before building the full object, I explored how different paper stocks behave. Understanding the material was a new part of the process and shaped how the layered structure could be made.

Build up the form in layers

The internal structure was made by layering cut paper stocks. Assembly involved checking the dimensions against the original and refining the edges, top and underside to create a consistent outer form.

05-06 / Fit · Cut

Check the stock against the object

Measuring and adjusting the stock helped keep each layer aligned with the charger's real proportions before the pieces were fixed together.

Prepare the layered core

Seven cut stock pieces formed the basis of the dock. Repeating the same outline built up the thickness while keeping the model's footprint consistent.

07-08 / Layer · Finish the edge

Join the cut stocks

Sticking the stocks together created the internal body of the dock. The layered construction was the most demanding part to keep precise and stable.

Wrap the layered structure

Covering the exposed edge helped turn the stacked core into a cleaner, more unified exterior while retaining the strength of its layered construction.

09-10 / Cover · Detail

Close the top and bottom

Covering both faces brought the main body closer to the charger's finished geometry and prepared it for its smaller details.

Make the logo

The logo was created with a laser cutter, bringing a separate fabrication technique into the hand-built paper model.

11-12 / Apply · Charging detail

Add the mark

Positioning the cut logo on the model helped bring the charger reference into the visual details of the prototype.

Make the charging section

The charger component was also prepared with the laser cutter, allowing its smaller geometry to be developed separately from the paper body.

13-14 / Refine · Assemble details

Cut and fit the parts

Cutting the charging details to size helped prepare them for fitting into the body and refining the overall shape.

Bring the details onto the model

The cut parts and logo were placed on the body, making it possible to check their fit and visual position against the full-size form.

15-16 / Join · Connector

Connect the two parts

Joining the separate pieces brought the main body and charging section together into a more complete dock prototype.

Shape the USB-C detail

The USB-C opening was carved into the model as one of the final details needed to match the reference object.

17-18 / Fit check · Complete the second part

Check the connector in place

Fitting the USB-C detail into the model checked how the cutout aligned with the charging section.

Make a cover for the charging section

Creating a cover for the second part helped bring the separate components together into a finished form.

19-20 / Final model · Reflection

Compare the result with the reference

The finished form brings together the layered paper body, logo and charging details, aiming to stay close to the real dimensions and geometry.

Learn by making

I had never built a paper-based model before, so learning how each material behaved was a real learning curve. The rounded parts and internal stick structure were the hardest to get right, but they taught me the most about precision, layering and stability. Measuring, adjusting, fixing, failing and retrying helped me understand the difference between imagining an object and actually constructing it. I am proud of how closely the model came to the real artifact and how much my hands-on skills improved through the process.
